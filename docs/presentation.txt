Slide 1 — Title
Good morning / afternoon, everyone. This presentation is about a hardware-software co-design project whose goal is to make a modern large-language-model run at conversational speed — meaning fast enough to hold a real-time spoken dialogue — on a small, embedded device that draws only a few watts of power. The work is a collaboration between the University of Trento and STMicroelectronics, and it sits at the intersection of three fields: edge computing, AI inference, and FPGA digital design. By the end I hope to convince you that, with the right choice of model and the right choice of what to accelerate, you can get surprisingly close to real-time performance on hardware that most people would not consider capable of running a billion-parameter language model.


Slide 2 — Agenda
The talk follows a natural engineering progression. We start with the motivation: why would you want to run a language model on an edge device at all, and how hard is that in practice? We then look at the state of the art to understand what others have done before. From there, we explain how we chose the specific model we worked with, and how we profiled it to understand where the computation actually goes. The second half of the talk covers the proposed system — how the FPGA accelerator is designed, how it interfaces with the CPU, and what performance it achieves. We close with a comparison against related work and a roadmap for future improvements.


Slide 3 — Background & Motivation (section divider)
This first section sets the stage. Before talking about any hardware, it is worth explaining why edge deployment of conversational AI is an interesting and non-trivial problem.


Slide 4 — Why Edge AI for Conversational Audio?
When you interact with a voice assistant today, your speech is almost certainly sent to a cloud server, processed there, and the response is streamed back. This works, but it creates three classes of problems. First, latency: even a fast network adds tens to hundreds of milliseconds of round-trip delay on top of the model's own compute time, and human perception of conversation is sensitive to delays beyond about 500 ms. Second, privacy: speech carries biometric information — your voice, your accent, your health state, the content of what you say — and sending it to an external server is a non-trivial data exposure. Third, reliability: a device that depends on a cloud connection loses its conversational capability in low-connectivity environments. Running the entire pipeline on-device eliminates all three problems. The challenge is that the models required for high-quality conversational audio — an automatic speech recognition front-end, a language model, and a text-to-speech back-end — are large and compute-hungry by embedded-hardware standards. The core question of this work is therefore: how close to real-time can we get, on modest hardware, with a state-of-the-art model?


Slide 5 — State of the Art (section divider)
To position the contribution, let us briefly survey what the community has done in related areas.


Slide 6 — From TinyML to Generative Edge AI
Edge AI has evolved in two distinct phases. The first phase, roughly 2020 to 2023, is what is usually called TinyML: deploying convolutional and recurrent neural networks on microcontrollers for tasks like keyword spotting, gesture recognition, or anomaly detection. These models are small — typically under a million parameters — and they fit in hundreds of kilobytes of memory. Since 2024, the edge has moved toward what we call Generative Edge AI: deploying quantized transformer-based models, also known as small language models or SLMs, that retain the reasoning and generation capabilities of large models while fitting within tighter resource envelopes. Techniques such as quantization (representing weights in 4 or 8 bits instead of 32), pruning, knowledge distillation, and hardware-aware architecture search have all contributed to making this possible. For the specific case of speech, the dominant pipeline remains modular: an ASR model converts audio to text, a language model generates a text response, and a TTS model synthesises the audio output. Each stage can be optimised independently. More recently, end-to-end models that fuse all three stages into a single network have appeared, reducing inter-stage latency at the cost of a larger single-model footprint. Platforms like the Raspberry Pi 4B have become the common evaluation platform for on-device speech, hosting ASR engines such as Faster-Whisper. But while these boards handle the ASR front-end, a capable conversational pipeline aggregates three or four models totalling several billion parameters, and the language-model stage is bound by memory bandwidth — so a commodity CPU struggles to meet the 200-to-500-millisecond per-stage latency budget. The binding constraint at the edge is latency and bandwidth, not raw power.


Slide 7 — Model Selection & Profiling (section divider)
Given this landscape, the first concrete decision is which model to target. This section describes how that choice was made systematically.


Slide 8 — Speech Model Profiling (GGUF)
Rather than picking a model by intuition, we profiled a representative set of speech and multimodal models to understand their deployment requirements quantitatively. The tool used — gguf-parser-go — extracts, from standardised model checkpoint files, the parameter count, RAM footprint, and estimated memory bandwidth demand. Figure 1 shows the result as a scatter plot: each bubble is a model, its position encodes parameter count and RAM usage, its size encodes estimated throughput in tera-operations per second. The key insight from this chart is that parameter count alone is a poor predictor of edge feasibility. Some models with 7 to 9 billion parameters exceed both the memory and bandwidth limits of embedded platforms despite offering strong conversational quality. To be deployable on a device drawing 3 to 10 watts with 4 GB of shared memory, a model needs to land in the lower-left region of this chart — small parameter count, low RAM, moderate bandwidth demand.


Slide 9 — The Choice of LFM2.5-Audio-1.5B-Q4
The profiling points clearly to one candidate: LFM2.5-Audio-1.5B-Q4, a model developed by Liquid AI. In its 4-bit quantized form it occupies only 0.29 GB of RAM — the smallest footprint among all models profiled — while requiring roughly 3.9 GB/s of memory bandwidth and offering a competitive 1.18 billion parameters. It is also an end-to-end architecture: a single model handles audio encoding, language modelling, and speech synthesis, eliminating the inter-stage handoff latency of a modular pipeline. Crucially, its 1.2-billion-parameter language backbone — the component that does the heavy reasoning — was explicitly co-designed for efficient CPU inference under 4-bit quantization. This makes it a natural, well-grounded starting point for hardware acceleration.


Slide 10 — CPU Execution Profile of the Language Backbone
Once the model is chosen, the next question is: where does the time go? To answer this, we instrumented the llama.cpp inference engine — the standard open-source CPU inference framework for these models — to measure how long each architectural block takes per token, on a quad-core ARM Cortex-A53 at 1.3 GHz. The results in Table 1 are stark. The MatMul + SwiGLU feed-forward block alone accounts for 68.1% of total inference time. The next largest contributors are the final linear projection (11%) and a gated short convolution specific to the LFM architecture (10.4%). Attention — which in larger models is often the dominant cost — contributes only 8.9% here, because for a 1.2B-parameter model the attention matrices are small relative to the feed-forward layers. This profiling result directly motivates the design strategy: rather than trying to accelerate every operation in the model, we focus exclusively on the feed-forward block. Amdahl's Law gives us an upper bound on the benefit: even if the feed-forward block ran instantaneously, the non-accelerated 32% of the workload caps the theoretical speedup at about 3.1×. In absolute terms, with the two CPU threads of our main operating point, everything outside the feed-forward block costs about 211 ms per token, so even an infinitely fast accelerator could not exceed about 4.7 tokens per second at two threads.


Slide 11 — Proposed System: FPGA Offload (section divider)
With the bottleneck identified, we can now describe the system we built to address it.


Slide 12 — Workload Partitioning
The system is heterogeneous: the FPGA handles the feed-forward MatMul + SwiGLU block for each of the 16 layers of the model, while the CPU handles everything else — attention, normalisation, residual additions, the gated short convolution, and the final linear projection. This partitioning is deliberate. Offloading only the dominant kernel means the FPGA design fits on a small device, the integration with the existing inference framework is surgical rather than a full rewrite, and CPU-only execution remains available with the same binary by setting one environment variable. The trade-off is that the non-accelerated operations become the new ceiling: about 211 ms per token at two threads, which is already more than the 131 ms the offloaded block now takes.


Slide 13 — Orchestration of the Computational Offload
The handoff between CPU and FPGA is managed inside a modified version of llama.cpp. When the engine builds the computation graph, it replaces the feed-forward block with a fused hardware operator — but only if the offload is enabled (LLAMA_SWIHW=1) and the gate, up and down weights are all in the Q4_K format; otherwise it builds the normal CPU graph. This is a graph-build decision, not a runtime fallback. At execution time, the sequence is as follows. First, the three weight matrices of the layer are rearranged into the accelerator's layout and copied into a 512 MiB DMA-capable shared buffer the first time the layer runs, then cached there permanently in a 30 MiB slot per layer: re-transferring about 30 MB of weights per layer per token would dominate the runtime. Second, the 2048-element activation vector is quantized from 32-bit floating point to 8-bit integers on the CPU, with one scale per token. Third, the CPU writes the buffer addresses and the scale into the FPGA's control registers over AXI-Lite, starts the IP, and sleeps until its interrupt arrives. The FPGA computes the whole feed-forward block, writes the 32-bit floating-point result back to the shared buffer, and raises the interrupt; the CPU copies the result to the next node of the graph. All of this CPU-side work — quantization, cache maintenance, register writes and the copy — costs about 1.6 ms per token across the 16 layers. Prompt processing uses the same path, one token per call.


Slide 14 — Accelerator Design (section divider)
Let us now go inside the FPGA and look at how the feed-forward computation is implemented.


Slide 15 — MatMul + SwiGLU Workload
The feed-forward block — the SwiGLU variant used here — involves three matrix-vector multiplications and a non-linear gating operation. The 2048-element input is projected to 8192 dimensions by two weight matrices (gate and up), a SiLU non-linearity is applied to the gate output, the two 8192-element vectors are multiplied element-wise, and the result is projected back to 2048 dimensions by the down matrix. The FPGA implements this as a five-stage HLS dataflow pipeline at 250 MHz. Stage 1 loads the INT8 input; Stages 2a and 2b compute the gate and up projections in parallel, each on its own DDR port; Stages 3 and 4 apply the gate and requantize it; Stage 5 computes the down projection. Weights stay in the 4-bit Q4_K format, the input and the gated activation are 8-bit integers, and the two intermediate projections are stored as 16-bit integers. Products accumulate in 32-bit integers, and each 256-weight block is rescaled in floating point. The key design step is overlap: in every multiply-accumulate stage, the weights of the next rows are loaded while the current rows are computed, so each iteration costs the longer of the two instead of their sum. Both projection stages became compute-bound as a result: Stages 2a/2b take 2.89 ms each, and Stage 5 takes 5.13 ms. A single call, from start to interrupt, takes 8.08 ms in synthesis and 8.09 ms on the board. Why is Stage 5 slower? The gate and up matrices contract over the 2048-element input, so each of their rows is 8 Q4_K blocks of 256 weights; the down matrix contracts over 8192 elements, so each row is 32 blocks. With four times longer rows and only 16 parallel lanes — halved from 32 so that the next rows' load fits behind the computation and to fund the overlap buffers — Stage 5 takes the larger share of the call.


Slide 16 — Bottlenecks
Two different limits now bound the accelerator. Stages 2a and 2b stream their weights at about 3.68 GB/s, which the DDR controller's performance monitor confirms on the board. That is 92% of the 4.0 GB/s that one 128-bit port can carry at 250 MHz, so these stages cannot get faster without more or wider ports. Stage 5 is bound by its multiply-accumulate array: its loads are hidden behind the computation, and the array is busy about 82% of the time. A single call performs about 50 million multiply-accumulates in 8.09 ms, or 6.22 billion per second (derived). This is far below the arithmetic peak of the DSP slices, but that is not a design failure. The weights of one layer are about 31 MB in the accelerator's layout, against about 3.4 MB of on-chip memory, so every token must stream them from DDR again. Autoregressive decoding processes one token at a time, so there is no batch to reuse the weights across. That is the memory-bound regime typical of language-model decoding.


Slide 17 — FPGA Resource Utilization on Kria KV260 (ZU5EV)
The target platform is the AMD-Xilinx Kria KV260, built around the Zynq UltraScale+ ZU5EV. It integrates a quad-core ARM Cortex-A53 and programmable fabric on the same die, sharing 4 GB of DDR4. After place and route, the accelerator and its interconnect use 86.7% of the LUTs, 60.2% of the flip-flops, 42.0% of the block RAM, 12.5% of the UltraRAM and 19.1% of the DSP slices; the accelerator alone accounts for about 97,000 of the 101,522 LUTs. The design meets timing at 250 MHz, but with little margin: 0.145 ns of worst-case slack. The low DSP figure does not mean the multipliers were left in LUTs. Each of the 80 multiply-accumulate lanes binds one DSP slice, which multiplies the 12-bit weight-times-activation product by the 8-bit sub-block scale; the narrow 4-by-8-bit product maps to LUTs, and the remaining DSPs serve the floating-point block rescaling. There are simply not many lanes: Stages 2a/2b are already at their port bandwidth, and Stage 5 was narrowed to 16 lanes to fund its overlap buffers. LUTs, not DSPs, are the scarce resource: at 87% there is no room for another kernel on this device.


Slide 18 — Experimental Results (section divider)
With the system fully described, we can now look at what it actually achieves.


Slide 19 — Throughput & Efficiency: FPGA+CPU vs CPU Only
Table 2 reports throughput, power and performance per watt across four thread counts (T1 to T4, one to four active CPU threads) for CPU Only and FPGA+CPU. Both configurations run the same binary and load the same model file: the Thinking variant in Q4_K_M with every down projection forced to Q4_K, because the accelerator decodes only Q4_K. The comparison therefore isolates the effect of the offload. Prefill is prompt processing, decode is the token-by-token generation. FPGA+CPU is faster at every thread count. In decode it reaches 1.94, 2.92, 3.53 and 3.24 tokens per second at T1 to T4, against 0.93, 1.76, 2.54 and 2.69 for CPU Only: speedups of 2.09, 1.66, 1.39 and 1.20 times. Comparing the best configuration of each path, FPGA+CPU at T3 gives 3.53 against 2.69 tokens per second at T4, a 1.31× gain. Board power is almost the same in both modes, 4.5 to 4.9 W, so FPGA+CPU also wins on efficiency at every thread count: decode efficiency is 0.433 to 0.717 tokens per second per watt, against 0.217 to 0.565, and the energy per generated token falls from 4.60 to 2.31 J at one thread and from 1.77 to 1.39 J between the best configurations (derived).


Slide 19b — Accuracy of the Accelerated Path (new slide)
A faster accelerator is only useful if it computes the same model. We therefore compared the FPGA+CPU output with CPU Only on the same model file: perplexity and the KL divergence of the next-token distribution on wikitext-2, and the per-layer cosine similarity against the floating-point CPU result. This check paid off twice. First, it exposed three driver bugs that the earlier throughput measurements had not revealed: a header layout mismatch between the host converter and the IP, activation buffers that overlapped one layer's weights, and an interrupt register that toggles on write. The earliest throughput figures had valid timing but wrong outputs. Second, with the driver fixed, the first IP stored the gate and up projections as 8-bit integers over a fixed ±10 range. The real values stay below about 4.3, so up to 2,347 of 8,192 values per layer rounded to zero, and the mean KL divergence against the CPU was 0.385. Widening those two intermediate results to 16 bits over ±32 costs 16 more BRAM blocks and no time. The mean KL divergence drops to 0.030, the perplexity ratio to 1.034, and the most likely next token agrees with the CPU 88% of the time instead of 63%. The remaining difference comes from the 8-bit input and gated activation. Against an unquantized f16/Q8_0 version of the model, the 4-bit weights alone cost a mean KL divergence of 0.166 on the CPU, close to the 0.153 of the standard Q4_K_M mix, and the accelerator raises it to 0.210: the IP's own 8-bit stages add about a quarter of what the weight quantization already costs. All throughput figures in this talk are for this accurate design.


Slide 20 — Results Analysis
The pattern in the data is simple. The FPGA time is fixed: 129.5 ms per token for the 16 calls at every thread count, plus about 1.6 ms of CPU-side handoff. Only the CPU part of the graph changes with the thread count: about 384, 211, 152 and 178 ms at T1 to T4 (derived). At one thread, the offload removes the largest piece of a slow token and the speedup is 2.09×. As threads are added, the CPU part shrinks, the fixed FPGA part becomes a larger share, and the gain falls to 1.20× at four threads. Both paths stop improving at four threads, and FPGA+CPU even gets slower (3.53 to 3.24 tokens per second) while its FPGA time is unchanged, so the loss is on the CPU side. We have not measured its cause. The practical implication is that the offload is most valuable when the surrounding pipeline — ASR and TTS — also needs CPU cores. FPGA+CPU at two threads (342 ms per token) is already faster than CPU Only at four (372 ms), which leaves two cores free for the other stages.


Slide 21 — Obtained Throughput
The board now runs exactly as fast as synthesis predicts: 8.09 ms per call in llama.cpp, 8.017 ms for a standalone call, against a synthesis latency of 8.08 ms. That was not true at first. The first board build of the overlapped design ran at 11.34 ms per call, 1.41 times its synthesis latency. A trace of the DDR controller's performance monitor showed that the excess was all in Stages 2a/2b: 745 cycles per iteration against the 348 that synthesis reported, while Stage 5 matched. A co-simulation with dataflow profiling then showed the cause. HLS synchronized each iteration of the overlapped loop on the variables it shared with the caller, so the next load waited for the previous computation; the synthesis report did not show this. Declaring the read-only inputs stable and writing results into a local array fixed it: Stage 2 dropped from 6.10 to 2.85 ms on the board and decode improved by 10 to 19%, with bit-identical output. The lesson is general: a synthesis interval is a schedule, not a measurement, and an overlapped loop must be checked in co-simulation or on the board. With the FPGA at 131 ms per token and the CPU part at 211 ms at two threads, the remaining ceiling is the CPU side: even an instantaneous accelerator would stop at about 4.7 tokens per second there.


Slide 22 — Discussion (section divider)
With the results in hand, let us now step back and interpret them in the context of the original goals.


Slide 23 — Real-Time Latency Compliance
Recall the latency target defined at the start: per-token generation time between 200 and 500 ms for the language model alone, so that the full pipeline (ASR + LM + TTS) can stay within the sub-second budget of responsive spoken dialogue. The table maps each operating point to its decode latency, the reciprocal of decode throughput. Of the eight KV260 configurations, five enter the sub-500 ms window: CPU Only at T3 and T4 (394 and 372 ms) and FPGA+CPU at T2, T3 and T4 (342, 283 and 309 ms). FPGA+CPU at one thread misses it narrowly at 515 ms, and no configuration reaches the 200 ms "instantaneous" threshold. FPGA+CPU reaches sub-500 ms with two threads, CPU Only needs three. This matters because a complete conversational pipeline must run ASR and TTS on the same four cores; the offload buys back cores for them. Even the fastest configuration, FPGA+CPU at T3, uses 283 ms of the 500 ms budget for the language model alone.


Slide 24 — Why Are the Gains Limited?
The gains are bounded at three levels. At the hardware level: the accelerator uses 86.7% of the LUTs and closes timing with only 0.145 ns of slack; each projection stage streams its weights through one 128-bit port at 92% of that port's bandwidth; and the weights are about nine times larger than all on-chip memory, so DDR streaming is unavoidable. At the micro-architecture level: Stage 5 runs 16 lanes, because a 32-lane array would idle while its four-row load runs, and a faster output path built on wide on-chip memories failed to route on this device. At the system level: the FPGA call and the CPU work run strictly one after the other, because each layer's output is the next layer's input. The handoff itself is cheap, about 1.6 ms per token, but the 211 ms of CPU work at two threads cannot overlap the 131 ms of FPGA work within a token. None of these comes from a poor design choice; they are the structural limits of selective acceleration on a device of this class, and they are quantified.


Slide 25 — Comparison with Other Work on the Same Platform
Three prior designs on the Kria KV260 are compared in Table 3. A Tiny Transformer for ECG classification (6,600 parameters) fits its entire pipeline on chip, so it never fetches weights from DDR and stays compute-bound — a fundamentally different situation from ours. A DistilBERT self-attention accelerator tiles its Q, K and V projections into on-chip block RAM and sustains 91% of its arithmetic peak with 83% DSP utilisation, which is possible only because those matrices fit the tiling footprint. The LFM2.5 feed-forward weights total about 31 MB per layer in our layout, roughly nine times the device's 3.4 MB of on-chip memory, so tiling is not an option. A BRAM-banked matrix-multiplication accelerator reaches 59% of its 22.83 GOps/s peak and identifies processor-side data handling as its main bottleneck. Our design reaches 6.22 billion multiply-accumulates per second over a whole call, at 86.7% LUT and 19% DSP. Its limits are the DDR port bandwidth in the projection stages and routing capacity in the down projection, not arithmetic.


Slide 26 — Comparison: KV260 vs STM32MP257F-DK
To understand the value of the FPGA fabric specifically, we also benchmarked LFM2.5-1.2B on the STM32MP257F-DK, an ST development board with a dual-core ARM Cortex-A35 at up to 1.5 GHz and no programmable accelerator. It ran the standard Q4_K_M model file, so the two platforms run closely related, not identical, models. At two threads the STM32 decodes 1.28 tokens per second, at an estimated total board power of 0.80 W (derived from datasheet currents, not measured). The KV260 with FPGA+CPU at two threads decodes 2.92 tokens per second — 2.28 times faster — but draws 4.78 W. In performance per watt the STM32 is 2.62 times more efficient: 1.60 against 0.611 tokens per second per watt. So why is the FPGA offload justified? Latency. The STM32 needs 781 ms per token at two threads, outside the 500 ms window. The KV260 FPGA+CPU path at two threads delivers 342 ms, well inside it. The offload is not justified by energy against a much smaller chip; it is justified because it reaches a latency target that this class of CPU cannot.


Slide 27 — Conclusions & Future Work (section divider)
Let us summarise what was achieved and where the work goes next.


Slide 28 — Conclusions
This work showed that selectively offloading the feed-forward MatMul + SwiGLU block of a 1.2-billion-parameter language model to an FPGA makes it faster than CPU-only execution of the same model at every thread count: 2.09× in decode at one thread, and 3.53 against 2.69 tokens per second when the best configuration of each path is compared. It also uses less energy per generated token at every thread count, and reaches sub-500 ms per-token latency with two CPU threads instead of three. These speeds come with their accuracy: against the CPU path, the mean KL divergence is 0.030 and the perplexity ratio 1.034, after validation exposed three driver bugs and an 8-bit intermediate format that had cost a divergence of 0.385. Overlapping weight loads with computation made every projection stage compute-bound, and once a hidden synchronization in the synthesized loops was removed, the board runs at exactly the synthesis latency. The integration hooks into llama.cpp through one fused operator, and the same binary runs CPU-only by setting one environment variable. The design uses 86.7% of the LUTs, and its projection stages use 92% of their port bandwidth, so further gains on this device must come from the CPU side of the graph rather than from more parallel hardware.


Slide 29 — Future Work & Roadmap
Five directions are identified. Memory bandwidth: Stages 2a/2b already read their weights at 92% of one 128-bit port, so a faster Stage 2 needs more or wider ports into DDR, and a faster Stage 5 needs a four-row load shorter than its computation, plus routing headroom that the ZU5EV does not have. The on-board DDR performance monitor that located the board/synthesis gap is the tool to check any such change. Numerical representation: the remaining difference from the CPU comes from the 8-bit input and gated activation, which add 0.044 of KL divergence against the unquantized model; widening them, or a per-token scale for the intermediate projections, are the next steps. The CPU side: at two threads the rest of the graph takes 211 ms per token, more than the 131 ms of the offloaded block, and the slowdown from three to four threads is still unexplained. Within one token the FPGA and CPU work cannot overlap, because each layer feeds the next. Overlapping them would need independent work, such as several concurrent sequences, and a change to ggml's node-by-node scheduler — which gives up some of the drop-in simplicity of the current integration. Spin-polling instead of interrupts was tested on an earlier design and gave little latency gain for more power. Hardware coverage and platform: the final linear projection (11%) and the gated short convolution (10%) are the next candidates, but there are no LUTs left on the ZU5EV. The ZU7EV, the next device of the same family, has about 230,000 LUTs against 117,000, room for a wider down-projection array and further kernels. Full pipeline integration: the goal remains co-deploying ASR, language model and TTS within the same power envelope, sharing the four CPU cores and the FPGA fabric without starving each other.
