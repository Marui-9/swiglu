\chapter{System Design and FPGA Accelerator}
\label{cha:design}

This chapter describes the proposed methodology. Section~\ref{sec:background} first
introduces the two architectural notions on which the rest of the chapter relies: the

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The Feed-Forward Block and SwiGLU}
\label{subsec:swiglu_bg}
Modern transformer language models alternate two kinds of sub-layer within each block:
a token-mixing operator (self-attention or, in LFM2, a short convolution) and a
position-wise feed-forward network (FFN). The FFN is applied independently to every
token's hidden vector and is responsible for most of a model's parameters and
arithmetic. A classic FFN expands the $d$-dimensional hidden vector to a larger
intermediate dimension $d_{\text{ff}}$ through one linear projection, applies a
pointwise nonlinearity, and projects back to $d$ through a second projection. Gated
Linear Unit (GLU) variants replace the single expansion with two parallel projections
combined by an element-wise gate, which improves quality at an equal parameter budget
and has become standard in recent large language models. LFM2 uses the SwiGLU variant.

SwiGLU combines a SiLU-gated branch with a linear branch. The SiLU (also called Swish)
activation is $\text{SiLU}(z) = z\,\sigma(z)$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid;
it is a smooth, non-monotonic function that acts as a soft gate, passing large positive
activations while suppressing negative ones. Given the input activation $x \in
\mathbb{R}^{d}$ (here $d = 2048$), SwiGLU computes a gate projection $X_1 = x W^{T}$ and
an up projection $X_2 = x V^{T}$, both mapping to the intermediate dimension
$d_{\text{ff}}$ (here $8192$); it applies SiLU to the gate, multiplies the two branches
element-wise, and projects the result back to $d$ with the down projection $W_{down}$:
\begin{equation}
\text{FFN}(x) = (\text{SiLU}(x W^{T}) \odot (x V^{T})) W_{down}^{T}
\label{eq:ffn}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. LFM2 applies no bias terms in the
FFN, which simplifies the arithmetic and is exploited by the hardware design in
Section~\ref{sec:fpga}.

The cost of this expressiveness is that SwiGLU uses three large weight matrices ($W$ and
$V$ of size $d \times d_{\text{ff}}$ and $W_{down}$ of size $d_{\text{ff}} \times d$)
rather than the two of a classic FFN. At $d = 2048$ and $d_{\text{ff}} = 8192$ each
matrix holds $2048 \times 8192 \approx 16.8$\,M weights, so a single block's FFN
contains roughly $50$\,M parameters and dominates both the storage footprint and the
multiply-accumulate count of the block. This is what makes the feed-forward path the
natural acceleration target identified in Section~\ref{subsec:cpuexec}.

\subsection{The LFM2 Hybrid Backbone}
\label{subsec:lfm2_bg}
LFM2 is a family of edge-first language models from Liquid AI, designed through a
hardware-in-the-loop architecture search that targets CPU latency and memory directly
rather than parameter count alone~\cite{amini2025lfm2technicalreport}. The resulting
backbone is a minimal hybrid: most blocks use an inexpensive gated short convolution
for token mixing, and only a small minority use grouped-query attention
(GQA)~\cite{ainslie2023gqa} to recover long-range modeling. This combination delivers up to roughly $2\times$ faster
prefill and decode on CPUs than similarly sized attention-only models, which is what
makes LFM2 a credible starting point for edge deployment.

The 1.2-billion-parameter backbone used in this work has the hyperparameters listed in
Table~\ref{tab:lfm2_dims}. It comprises 16 transformer blocks over a hidden dimension
of 2048, of which 10 are gated short-convolution blocks and 6 are GQA blocks; every
block, regardless of its mixer, contains a SwiGLU FFN that expands to a feed-forward
dimension of 8192.

Internally, each block follows the pre-norm residual structure illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:lfm2_block}: the hidden state is normalized with RMSNorm, passed
through the sequence mixer, and added back to the residual stream; it is then
normalized again, passed through the SwiGLU FFN, and added back. The sequence mixer is
either a gated short convolution (a depthwise causal convolution of kernel size
$k = 3$ surrounded by input-dependent multiplicative gating, which provides cheap local
mixing with favourable cache behaviour on CPUs) or grouped-query attention, which
shares key and value projections across groups of query heads (here 32 query heads over
8 key/value groups) to reduce key--value cache traffic, augmented with rotary position
embeddings and query--key normalization.

Two properties of this structure shape the rest of the chapter. First, because a SwiGLU
FFN appears in all 16 blocks, the feed-forward path aggregates to the single largest
share of per-token computation, as the profiling in Section~\ref{subsec:cpuexec}
confirms. Second, the FFNs are never adjacent: consecutive feed-forward blocks are
always separated by a full sequence mixer and two normalization and residual steps. In
the heterogeneous design of Section~\ref{sec:orchestration}, those intervening operators
remain on the CPU, so the accelerator cannot chain one FFN directly into the next;
instead, every generated token incurs sixteen separate CPU--FPGA handoffs, one per
block.

\section{Profiling under CPU Execution}
\label{subsec:cpuexec}

To address the research question of enabling conversational-quality inference within
the constraints of edge devices, this work adopts a systems-driven, top-down
methodology that combines model profiling with targeted hardware acceleration. Rather
than attempting to accelerate the entire conversational pipeline, the approach focuses
on identifying the computational bottlenecks of the language backbone that powers the
selected speech model and mapping the most demanding operations to dedicated hardware.

The starting point of the approach is the LFM2-Audio architecture, whose
conversational capabilities are supported by a 1.2\,B-parameter language backbone. To
understand its execution characteristics on embedded hardware, the inference engine
implemented by llama.cpp was modified to instrument the execution of individual
architectural blocks. This profiling allows the collection of fine-grained timing
information for each major component of the model during inference.

Table~\ref{tab:cpu_profile} summarizes the resulting execution profile on a quad-core
Arm\textregistered{} Cortex\textregistered{}-A53 platform. These results indicate that
the performance of the model is primarily determined by a small set of dense linear
algebra operations. In particular, the feed-forward MatMul + SwiGLU blocks represent
the principal computational bottleneck, followed by the projection layers and
attention-related matrix multiplications. Since these operations exhibit regular and
predictable memory access patterns, they are well suited for hardware acceleration via
spatial architectures that can exploit custom dataflow and data reuse.

Amdahl's Law~\cite{Amdahl1967ValidityOT} bounds the theoretical benefit of this
strategy: with the feed-forward MatMul + SwiGLU block (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ffn}) accounting
for approximately 68\% of total execution time, the maximum achievable system speedup
is limited to $1/(1-0.68) \approx 3.1\times$, even if the accelerated portion were to
execute instantaneously. The absolute size of the non-accelerated remainder depends on
the thread count and on the llama.cpp build. An early experiment that replaced the
accelerator with a pass-through IP measured about 126\,ms per token outside the FFN, but
on an older CPU build and at an unrecorded thread count. With the build, model, and two
threads used for the measurements of Chapter~\ref{cha:experiments}, the rest of the
graph (attention, short convolution, normalizations, residual additions, final
projection, and sampling) costs about 211\,ms per generated token (derived in
Section~\ref{sec:results}). At that operating point, even an instantaneous FFN would cap
decode at about $1000/211 \approx 4.7$ tokens per second.

Based on this analysis, the proposed system focuses on offloading the most
computationally intensive component of the model to an FPGA-based accelerator while
leaving control logic and lightweight operations on the CPU. This selective
acceleration strategy reduces per-token latency at every thread count tested
(Chapter~\ref{cha:experiments}) without requiring a full hardware implementation of the
model.

\section{Orchestration of the Computation Handoff}
\label{sec:orchestration}

Following the identification of the dominant computational bottleneck, the llama.cpp
inference engine was extended with a hardware offloading mechanism that intercepts the
execution of the feed-forward MatMul + SwiGLU block and orchestrates a handoff to a
custom accelerator implemented in the FPGA fabric. Inference is executed using a
modified \texttt{llama.cpp} CPU backend. Model weights are loaded from GGUF, and for
each token a GGML computation graph is built (attention, norms, FFN). The graph is
executed node-by-node via \texttt{ggml\_graph\_compute}, which dispatches each
operation to CPU kernels by default.

To accelerate the dominant FFN block, the LFM2 builder replaces the standard FFN
sequence with a fused operator (\texttt{ggml\_swiglu\_fused\_hw}) when the environment
variable \texttt{LLAMA\_SWIHW=1} is set and the gate, up, and down weights are all
Q4\_K. The layer index is stored in the node's operator parameters and selects the
correct weights. At
execution time, this fused node triggers a custom kernel that manages CPU--FPGA
interaction. On a layer's first call, its three weight matrices are converted to the
accelerator's layout (Section~\ref{subsec:predecode}) and cached in a dedicated
30\,MiB slot of a physically contiguous, DMA-coherent buffer (a 512\,MiB udmabuf)
accessible by both the CPU and the FPGA. They are never re-transferred after that. The
current activation vector is quantized from FP32 to INT8 on the CPU with a per-token
scale $\max|x|/127$. The CPU programs the accelerator via memory-mapped registers,
asserts a start signal, and sleeps until an interrupt from the FPGA fabric signals
completion. The FPGA computes the full MatMul + SwiGLU block in a pipelined dataflow
and writes the FP32 result back to the DMA buffer, from which the CPU copies it into
the GGML output tensor. All other layer operations remain on the CPU. Prefill and
batched evaluation are handled by looping over the tokens, one IP call per token. If
\texttt{LLAMA\_SWIHW} is unset or 0, or the weights are not all Q4\_K, the graph is
built with the standard CPU FFN instead. The decision is taken once, at graph-build
time; there is no per-call fallback inside the fused kernel, which treats any other
weight type as an error.

\begin{algorithm}[htb!]
\caption{LFM2.5 Inference Execution}
\label{alg:inference}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE load\_model(GGUF)
\FOR{each token}
    \STATE $graph \leftarrow$ build\_ggml\_graph(token)
    \FOR{each node in $graph$}
        \IF{node.op == SWIGLU\_FUSED\_HW and enabled}
            \STATE // CPU--FPGA handoff
            \IF{not $weights\_cached[layer]$}
                \STATE Pre-decode $W_{gate}, W_{up}, W_{down} \rightarrow shared\_mem[layer]$
                \STATE Mark cached
            \ENDIF
            \STATE $x_{int8}, x_{scale} =$ quantize($x$)
            \STATE Write $x_{int8} \rightarrow shared\_mem$
            \STATE program\_fpga($W_{addrs}, x_{addr}, out_{addr}, mode, x_{scale}$) \COMMENT{write AXI-Lite ctrl regs}
            \STATE start\_fpga()
            \STATE wait\_for\_interrupt()
            \STATE $out =$ read\_output($shared\_mem$)
        \ELSE
            \STATE $out =$ cpu\_kernel(node)
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \COMMENT{graph execution complete; proceed to next token}
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Although Algorithm~\ref{alg:inference} expresses the offload as a single conditional
inside the per-node loop, its effect over a full token is dictated by the block
structure of Section~\ref{subsec:lfm2_bg}. The computation graph of one token contains
sixteen SwiGLU FFN nodes, one per transformer block, and the driver intercepts every
one of them. The offload is therefore not a single event but a sequence of sixteen
separate, synchronous CPU--FPGA handoffs per generated token.

These handoffs cannot be fused or chained on the FPGA, because consecutive FFNs are
never adjacent in the graph. The output of the FFN in block $N$ is consumed by the
residual addition, the RMSNorm, and the sequence mixer of block $N{+}1$, all of which
execute on the CPU; only their result becomes the input activation of the FFN in block
$N{+}1$. The accelerator thus has no way to advance from one FFN to the next on its own:
the CPU lies on the critical path between every pair of feed-forward blocks. As a
consequence the two devices strictly alternate: while the FPGA evaluates one FFN the CPU
is idle waiting on the completion interrupt, and while the CPU computes a block's mixer
and normalizations the FPGA is idle, so neither device overlaps the other within a token.
Figure~\ref{fig:handoff_mechanism} details the protocol of a single handoff.

Each of the sixteen handoffs carries a fixed overhead that is independent of the work it
guards: the input activation is quantized to INT8, the AXI-Lite control registers are
programmed, the shared buffer is synchronized for the device, the calling thread blocks
until the completion interrupt, and the result is copied back into the GGML tensor. This
cost is paid sixteen times per token and does not amortize with sequence length, since
decode generates one token at a time. It is, however, small. The driver's per-token
timers (enabled with \texttt{SWIGLU\_DEBUG=1}) report 129.5--129.6\,ms per token
between \texttt{ap\_start} and the interrupt, and 131.1--131.3\,ms for the whole FFN
path including quantization, cache synchronization, and the output copy, at every
thread count. The handoff itself therefore costs under 2\,ms per token (derived). The
dominant costs are the FPGA execution and the remaining CPU graph, which
Section~\ref{sec:results} breaks down. What the synchronous handoff does cost is
concurrency: the CPU is idle while the FPGA runs and the FPGA is idle while the CPU
computes the mixer and normalizations. Overlapping the two would require an
asynchronous, pipelined handoff in place of the node-by-node, barrier-synchronized
execution used here; this direction, and the compatibility trade-off it entails, is
discussed in Chapter~\ref{cha:conclusioni}.

\subsection{Driver Correctness}
\label{subsec:driver_bugs}
The first measured output accuracy of the integrated system (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy})
exposed three driver bugs that throughput measurements alone could not reveal. All three
were in the host driver, so fixing them required no re-synthesis:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Header layout.} The host converter wrote the eight 6-bit sub-block
    scales at header bytes 4--11 and the eight minima at 12--19, whereas the IP reads
    scales 0--3 at bytes 4--7, minima 0--3 at 8--11, scales 4--7 at 16--19 and minima 4--7
    at 20--23. The C-simulation testbench used its own converter, which matched the IP,
    so the mismatch was invisible in simulation. On a C model of the datapath, fixing the
    layout moved the output cosine similarity against a floating-point reference from
    $-0.196$ to $0.996$.
    \item \textbf{Buffer overlap.} The activation and output buffers lay inside layer~3's
    weight slot and overwrote 9 of its 8192 gate rows on every call. They now sit below
    the first layer slot, and compile-time assertions check the whole buffer map for
    overlaps.
    \item \textbf{Interrupt status register.} The status register is toggle-on-write. An
    unconditional write of 1 at initialization set the bit on a freshly loaded bitstream,
    so the first call returned early. The driver now writes back only the bits it reads
    as set.
\end{enumerate}
The lesson is methodological: a testbench and an IP that agree, and a host converter and
its own model that agree, prove nothing about the host and the IP together. After the
fixes, a per-layer check compares the IP output on the board with a bit-accurate C
emulation of its integer stages (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

\section{FPGA Accelerator}
\label{sec:fpga}

The proposed accelerator implements the SwiGLU feed-forward block of the LFM2.5 language
backbone,
\begin{equation}
\text{FFN}(x) = \big(\text{SiLU}(x W^{T}) \odot (x V^{T})\big)\, W_{down}^{T},
\label{eq:ffn_hw}
\end{equation}
which the five-stage dataflow pipeline evaluates through the decomposition:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:swiglu_stage_set}
\begin{align}
X_1 &= x W^{T} \label{eq:swiglu_s2a}\\
X_2 &= x V^{T} \label{eq:swiglu_s2b}\\
G   &= \text{SiLU}(X_1)\odot X_2 \label{eq:swiglu_s3}\\
\text{FFN}(x) &= G W_{down}^{T} \label{eq:swiglu_s4}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $x$ is the input activation, $W$ is the gating projection, $V$ is the expansion
projection, and $W_{down}$ is the output projection; $\odot$ is element-wise
multiplication and $\text{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \sigma(z)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:swiglu_s2a}
is computed in Stage~2a (gate projection), Eq.~\eqref{eq:swiglu_s2b} in Stage~2b (up
projection), Eq.~\eqref{eq:swiglu_s3} in Stages~3--4 (gated activation: Stage~3
computes $\text{SiLU}(X_1)\odot X_2$ and its maximum, Stage~4 recomputes it and quantizes
it to INT8), and Eq.~\eqref{eq:swiglu_s4} in Stage~5 (down
projection).

The design exposes AXI4 interfaces for weights and activations, and an AXI-Lite
interface for runtime control (buffer addresses and the per-token activation scale). The
five-stage pipeline is organized as an HLS dataflow (Fig.~\ref{fig:swiglu_compact}).
Within each projection stage, a second, loop-level dataflow overlaps the DDR load of the
next group of weight rows with the multiply-accumulate (MAC) on the current one, so the
per-iteration cost is the larger of the two rather than their sum. After this overlap,
both projection stages are bound by their MAC arrays, not by DDR, although the gate and
up loads already use about 92\% of their ports
(Sections~\ref{subsec:stage2}--\ref{subsec:stage5}).

Since the decode loop calls the IP once per layer and waits for the result before the
CPU can continue, a call costs the full pipeline \emph{latency}, which is the sum of the
stage latencies along the dependency chain:
$202 + 721{,}303 + 16{,}622 + 1{,}282{,}062$ cycles plus three cycles of
handshake, i.e.\ 2,020,192 cycles, or 8.08\,ms at 250\,MHz. The dataflow \emph{interval} (1,282,063 cycles, set by Stage~5) would govern
only a stream of independent calls, which single-token decode never issues.

\subsection{HLS Realization}
\label{subsec:hls}

The accelerator is written in C++ and compiled to register-transfer-level hardware by
Vitis HLS. Its functional behaviour is expressed in ordinary loops and arithmetic, while
the mapping to hardware (interface protocols, on-chip memories, parallelism, and
pipelining) is steered by directives, or pragmas, that annotate the code without altering
its result. Listing~\ref{lst:hls} shows the top-level function, which both defines the
IP's external interface and launches the five pipeline stages.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=hls, float=htb, caption={Condensed top-level HLS function: the external interface and the five-stage dataflow, followed by the loop-level dataflow inside Stage~5. Repeated \texttt{m\_axi} and \texttt{s\_axilite} lines and argument lists are collapsed for clarity.}, label=lst:hls]
void swiglu(const uint8_t *W, const uint8_t *V, const uint8_t *W_down,
            const int8_t *x_batch, float *out_batch,
            uint32_t down_quant_mode, float x_scale) {
  // weight/activation pointers become 128-bit AXI4 master ports to DDR
  #pragma HLS INTERFACE m_axi port=W bundle=gmem_W max_widen_bitwidth=128
  // ... V, W_down, x_batch, out_batch likewise ...
  // scalar arguments become memory-mapped control registers
  #pragma HLS INTERFACE s_axilite port=x_scale bundle=CTRL
  // ... addresses, down_quant_mode, ap_ctrl likewise ...

  int16_t X1_cache[FFN_DIM];       // X2_cache likewise
  #pragma HLS BIND_STORAGE    variable=X1_cache   type=ram_2p impl=bram
  int8_t gate_cache[DOWN_BLOCKS][256];
  #pragma HLS BIND_STORAGE    variable=gate_cache type=ram_1p impl=uram
  #pragma HLS ARRAY_PARTITION variable=gate_cache dim=2 cyclic factor=8

  #pragma HLS DATAFLOW                          // stages become processes; 2a || 2b
  load_x_local(x_batch, x_local_1, x_local_2);
  compute_X1(W, x_local_1, x_scale, X1_cache);              // Stage 2a
  compute_X2(V, x_local_2, x_scale, X2_cache);              // Stage 2b
  compute_gate(X1_cache, X2_cache, gate_cache, gate_scale); // Stages 3-4
  compute_output(W_down, gate_cache, gate_scale, out_batch,
                 down_quant_mode);                          // Stage 5
}

// inside compute_output (compute_X1/X2: same structure, K=4, plus STABLE)
DOWN_Q4K: for (int out_i = 0; out_i < VECTOR_DIM; out_i += 2) {
  #pragma HLS DATAFLOW                          // load of rows i+2,i+3 overlaps MAC of i,i+1
  ap_uint<32> g0r0[256], /* ... 8 nibble tiles, headers ... */;
  load_2rows_down(W_down_wide, out_i, g0r0, /* ... */);   // producer
  mac_write_down(g0r0, /* ... */, gate_cache, out_local); // consumer
}
\end{lstlisting}

Four kinds of directive appear. The \texttt{m\_axi} pragmas turn the weight and
activation pointers into AXI4 master ports. Since HLS by default infers the bus width
from the \texttt{uint8\_t} element type, giving an 8-bit bus, the
\texttt{max\_widen\_bitwidth=128} option is what widens each port to 128 bits, the width
of the device's PS--PL high-performance interface, and raises its achievable DDR
bandwidth roughly sixteen-fold relative to the 8-bit default. Widening the port further brings no benefit, since it cannot exceed that 128-bit interface width. 
The \texttt{s\_axilite} pragmas expose the
scalar arguments (the weight and buffer addresses, the per-token activation scale, a
mode register that is always 0 but is kept so that the register offsets stay fixed, and
the \texttt{ap\_ctrl} start/done handshake) as the memory-mapped control
registers that the driver programs during the handoff of
Section~\ref{sec:orchestration}. The \texttt{BIND\_STORAGE} directives pin each on-chip
array to a specific primitive, LUTRAM, BRAM, or URAM, so that the design fits the
ZU5EV's mixed memory budget; \texttt{X1\_cache} and \texttt{X2\_cache} are dual-port
(\texttt{ram\_2p}) BRAM arrays of 8192 INT16 values, written by the projection stages and
read by \texttt{compute\_gate}. The two copies of the input activation
(\texttt{x\_local\_1}, \texttt{x\_local\_2}) live in LUTRAM; a single shared copy would make
the tool serialize the two projection stages. \texttt{ARRAY\_PARTITION} with
\texttt{cyclic factor=8} splits the gate cache into eight URAM banks so that eight blocks
can be read in a single cycle, feeding the parallel MAC lanes. Finally, \texttt{DATAFLOW} turns the five stage
functions into separate hardware processes connected by ping-pong buffers. This is what
realizes the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:swiglu_compact}: the two projection stages run
in parallel, and each later stage starts when the buffers it reads are complete. Within
one call the chain is therefore sequential, and successive stages would overlap only
across a stream of independent calls, which decode does not issue
(Section~\ref{sec:fpga}).

The remaining directives operate inside the stage functions. Each MAC loop is pipelined
at an initiation interval of one (\texttt{PIPELINE II=1}), with the block and row loops
unrolled inside it (\texttt{UNROLL}), so the partitioned weight and activation banks are
consumed in parallel. This gives 32 MAC lanes in each of Stages~2a and~2b and 16 in
Stage~5. The outer row loop of each projection stage carries its own \texttt{DATAFLOW}
directive, shown at the end of Listing~\ref{lst:hls}: HLS turns the weight tiles declared
inside the loop body into ping-pong (PIPO) buffers, so the load of the next rows fills
one buffer while the MAC reads the other. In Stages~2a/2b this overlap also needs
\texttt{STABLE} directives and a local result array, without which the iterations
serialize on the board although csynth reports them as overlapped
(Section~\ref{subsec:stage2}).

Two HLS pitfalls shaped this structure; both inflated the csynth LUT estimate before they
were found. First, an array that is completely partitioned and crosses a dataflow
boundary becomes one handshake channel per element: the $32\times8$ INT8 sub-block scales
of a down-projection row produced about 1,150 channels (+77\,K LUT, +114\,K FF). Packing
the eight scales of a block into one 64-bit word reduces this to about 40 channels.
Second, a variable shift such as \texttt{x >> (sub*8)} synthesizes a barrel shifter at
every use (about 160 LUTs at each of 96 sites, +16\,K LUT); a fully unrolled compile-time
\texttt{.range()} unpack into partitioned locals is pure wiring.

\subsection{Quantization}

\label{subsec:quant}
The weights use the Q4\_K format of llama.cpp. Each 256-weight super-block carries two
fp16 scales ($d$, $dmin$), and each of its eight 32-weight sub-blocks carries a 6-bit
scale and a 6-bit minimum, so a weight decodes as $d\cdot sc\cdot q - dmin\cdot m$ with a
4-bit $q$. This asymmetric, two-level scaling is finer than the symmetric Q4\_0 format
that Liquid AI uses for its published LFM2 CPU benchmarks~\cite{amini2025lfm2technicalreport}.

The default Q4\_K\_M distribution of LFM2.5-1.2B stores the down-projection of eight
layers (0, 1, 4, 7, 10, 13, 14, 15) as Q6\_K. The accelerator supports Q4\_K only: a
210-byte Q6\_K block (13.125 128-bit words) does not admit the tile layout that makes
nibble extraction free (Section~\ref{subsec:predecode}), and an earlier Q6\_K datapath
was removed to save LUTs. The model is therefore quantized so that every FFN matrix is
Q4\_K: the Thinking variant's f16/q8\_0 GGUF was converted with
\texttt{llama-quantize ... Q4\_K\_M -{-}tensor-type ffn\_down=q4\_k}. This required a
local patch to llama-quantize, because the build used ignores an override equal to the
type's default and would otherwise keep Q6\_K. The resulting file (661.76\,MiB as
reported by \texttt{llama-bench}) is used for \emph{both} the CPU Only and the FPGA+CPU
measurements of Chapter~\ref{cha:experiments}, so the two paths run identical weights.
The integration enforces Q4\_K at three levels: the graph builder emits the fused
operator only for Q4\_K weights, the driver rejects any other type, and the IP contains
no other decoder. The quality cost of Q4\_K instead of Q6\_K on those eight
down-projections is small: on the CPU, the mean KL divergence against an f16/Q8\_0
version of the model rises from 0.153 to 0.166 (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

The rest of the datapath is integer wherever the MACs are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the input $x$ is INT8 with a per-token scale $\max|x|/127$ computed on the CPU;
    \item the MACs multiply the INT8 activation by the 4-bit weight and the 6-bit
    sub-block scale, and accumulate in INT32 (four interleaved accumulators per lane, to
    cover the adder latency);
    \item each 256-weight block is reduced as $d\,\Sigma_w - dmin\,\Sigma_m$ in floating
    point and accumulated in a 48-bit fixed-point register;
    \item $X_1$ and $X_2$ are stored as INT16 over a fixed $\pm32$ range (step
    $32/32767 \approx 9.8\times10^{-4}$, round half away from zero, saturating);
    \item the gate $h = \text{SiLU}(X_1)\odot X_2$ uses a 4096-entry sigmoid table over
    $[-8, 8)$ and is stored as INT8 with a per-token scale found in a first pass;
    \item the output is FP32.
\end{itemize}

The $X_1$/$X_2$ format is the result of a measured accuracy problem. The first version
stored them as INT8 over a fixed $\pm10$ range (step $\approx 0.079$). On the board, the
real values stayed within $|X_1|\le4.24$ and $|X_2|\le1.80$, so 801--2,347 of the 8192
gate values per layer rounded to zero; the per-layer cosine similarity against the
floating-point FFN dropped to 0.957, and the mean KL divergence of the next-token
distribution against the CPU reached 0.385. Widening $X_1$/$X_2$ to INT16 over $\pm32$
leaves 10--38 values per layer below half a step and reduces the mean KL divergence to
0.030 (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}). A per-token $X_1$/$X_2$ scale would have reached
similar accuracy in emulation but needs a second pass over the 8192 values; the
fixed INT16 range does not, and costs only 16 additional BRAM\_18K in csynth (150 to
166).

\subsection{CPU-Side Weight Pre-Decoding}
\label{subsec:predecode}

Each MAC stage processes $K$ weight rows per iteration across eight Q4\_K blocks in
parallel: $K=4$ in the projection stages (32 INT32 MAC lanes, 8 blocks $\times$ 4 rows)
and $K=2$ in the down-projection (16 lanes, 8 blocks $\times$ 2 rows, applied to the 32
blocks of a row in 4 sequential groups). Sustaining this degree of parallelism within the
device's LUT budget is made possible by a pre-decoding step that runs on the CPU, once
per layer, before the weights are ever seen by the accelerator.

The difficulty stems from the Q4\_K storage format, which is designed for CPU SIMD
execution rather than for an FPGA datapath. Each 144-byte block encodes 256 weights as
shown in Table~\ref{tab:q4k_block}: two fp16 block-level scales ($d$ and $dmin$), eight
6-bit sub-block scales and eight 6-bit minima (\texttt{sc6}, \texttt{mn6}) packed at
bit-level offsets that straddle byte boundaries, and 256 four-bit nibbles stored in
block-major order. Decoding this layout inside the MAC pipeline would require, for every
nibble, a \texttt{get\_byte()} access, a nine-to-one multiplexer on the 128-bit word
followed by a four-bit shift. With eight blocks decoded in parallel, these multiplexer
trees would dominate the LUT budget of the MAC stages.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Layout of a 144-byte Q4\_K block (256 weights).}
\label{tab:q4k_block}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
\textbf{Bytes} & \textbf{Size} & \textbf{Field} \\ \hline
0--1    & 2\,B   & $d$ (fp16 block scale) \\
2--3    & 2\,B   & $dmin$ (fp16 min-subtraction scale) \\
4--11   & 8\,B   & \texttt{sc6}/\texttt{mn6} sub-block scales and minima (low 6 bits) \\
12--15  & 4\,B   & \texttt{sc6}/\texttt{mn6} high-order bits (interleaved) \\
16--143 & 128\,B & 256 packed 4-bit nibbles (block-major) \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The pre-decoding step performs two transformations that move all of this irregular bit
manipulation off the critical path and onto the CPU. First, each block's header is
rewritten into two 128-bit words in which every field sits at a fixed byte: $d$ and
$dmin$ in bytes 0--3, the eight 6-bit scales and minima unpacked to one byte each
(scales 0--3 in bytes 4--7, minima 0--3 in 8--11, scales 4--7 in 16--19, minima 4--7 in
20--23). The FPGA then reads each sub-block scale as a plain byte. Getting this exact
layout to agree between host and IP was the first of the driver bugs of
Section~\ref{subsec:driver_bugs}. Second, and more importantly, the nibbles are
transposed from the block-major order in which Q4\_K stores them into an element-major
order, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:predecode}: instead of all 256 nibbles of block~0
followed by all 256 of block~1, the pre-decoded layout places the nibble of a given
element for all eight blocks side by side in one 32-bit slice. In the gate and up
matrices, a 128-bit word holds four such slices (four consecutive elements), so a row
of eight blocks is 16 header words plus 64 nibble words (80 words, 1,280\,B against
1,152\,B in raw Q4\_K). In the down matrix, whose rows have 32 blocks, a 128-bit word
holds one element for all four groups of eight blocks, so a row is 64 header words plus
256 nibble words (320 words). Both layouts make each matrix exactly 10\,MiB
(10,485,760\,B). The fp16 scales $d$ and $dmin$ are copied verbatim and converted to fp32
in hardware by a custom bit-manipulation routine that preserves subnormal values rather
than flushing them to zero.

After this rearrangement the FPGA load is trivial. In the gate and up stages each
128-bit DDR word is written verbatim into a wide (128-bit) BRAM tile, one write per
cycle; in the down stage each word is split into four 32-bit tiles, one per group, still
at an initiation interval of one (csynth). In the MAC, the nibbles are addressed by
compile-time \texttt{.range()} slices, which synthesize to wires. Without the
multiplexer trees the design fits the ZU5EV: the routed implementation occupies 86.7\%
of the LUTs (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The pre-decode itself was estimated at about
10\,ms per layer on the first token in an earlier version of the converter (not
re-measured for the current one); its output is cached permanently in the shared
udmabuf buffer, so every subsequent token reads the transposed weights directly and
pays nothing.

\subsection{Stage 1: Load $x$ (INT8)}
The 2048-element INT8 input vector is burst-loaded from DDR in $128\times$128-bit AXI
words and duplicated into two identically partitioned LUTRAM buffers, enabling
concurrent access by Stages~2a and~2b without serialization. The stage is pure data
movement with no arithmetic and completes in 202 cycles (0.81\,$\mu$s).

\subsection{Stages 2a / 2b: Linear Projections (Parallel)}
\label{subsec:stage2}
Two GEMV operations compute $X_1$ and $X_2$
(Eqs.~\eqref{eq:swiglu_s2a}--\eqref{eq:swiglu_s2b}) in parallel, each on its own AXI
port (HP2 and HP0) and its own on-chip buffers. Each iteration of the outer loop handles
$K=4$ rows. A producer function fetches the four rows in one merged 320-word AXI burst
($4\times80$ words) and stores the nibble words verbatim in four 64-word, 128-bit BRAM
tiles; a consumer function runs the 32-lane MAC (8 blocks $\times$ 4 rows, 256 cycles at
$II=1$) and the per-block reduction, and writes four INT16 results to the $X$ cache.

Because the loop body is a dataflow region, the load of rows $i{+}4..i{+}7$ runs while
the MAC works on rows $i..i{+}3$. The csynth report gives a load interval of 320 cycles
and a MAC interval of 347 cycles, so an iteration costs 348 cycles and the stage is
MAC-bound, with the two halves nearly balanced. Over 2048 iterations the loop takes
713,106 cycles. The MAC writes its results into a local array, which a final loop copies
into the $X$ cache in 8,193 cycles, so each stage takes 721,303 cycles (2.89\,ms at
250\,MHz), against 1,536,001 cycles (6.14\,ms) for the previous version, in which load
and MAC ran back to back. Over the loop, each stage streams its 10\,MiB matrix at about
3.68\,GB/s, or 92\% of the 4.0\,GB/s peak of a 128-bit port at 250\,MHz, and performs
$16.8$\,M MACs at about $5.9\times10^{9}$ MAC/s, or 74\% of the 32-lane peak (all
derived).

\textbf{Making the overlap real.} The first board build of this design ran each call
1.41 times slower than its csynth latency (11.34 against 8.05\,ms), where the previous
design had a ratio of 1.10. A trace of the DDR controller's built-in AXI performance
monitor during a single call located the excess in Stages~2a/2b: they took 6.10\,ms,
745 cycles per iteration (derived), while csynth reported 348; Stage~5 took 5.06\,ms,
in line with its csynth schedule. A C/RTL co-simulation of a reduced design (32
iterations) with dataflow profiling reproduced the problem: each load started only after
the previous MAC had finished, giving 746 cycles per iteration: a 401-cycle load
followed by a 343-cycle MAC. HLS synchronizes successive instances of a loop-body dataflow
region on the caller's variables that the region accesses (here the weight pointer, the
activation copy, the activation scale and the $X$ cache), and the csynth interval does
not show this. The weight pointer, activation copy and scale do not change during a
call, so they are marked \texttt{STABLE}, and the results go to the local array
described above instead of the $X$ cache. Co-simulation then gives 344 cycles per
iteration, and the board trace 348; the whole call takes 8.017\,ms, within 1\% of the
csynth latency. Marking the $X$ cache itself \texttt{STABLE} also removed the
synchronization, but in co-simulation 16 of 256 outputs read stale values, so that
variant was discarded. Stage~5 has the same loop structure but was never affected: HLS
infers stability for its inputs, and it already writes a local output array.

\subsection{Stages 3--4: Gated Activation and Quantization}
The fused gate operation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:swiglu_s3}) runs entirely on-chip from the
$X_1$ and $X_2$ caches using a two-pass scheme over 8,192 elements. The first pass
computes $\text{SiLU}(X_1)\odot X_2$ and finds its maximum absolute value across 8
parallel reduction lanes (8,254 cycles); the second recomputes it and quantizes it to
INT8 with the scale $\max/127$ for storage in the 8-way banked URAM gate cache (8,273
cycles). With 95 cycles outside the two loops, the stage takes 16,622 cycles
(66\,$\mu$s) in csynth and contributes under 1\% of the call
latency.

\subsection{Stage 5: Down Projection}
\label{subsec:stage5}
The gated activation is projected back to model dimension (Eq.~\eqref{eq:swiglu_s4}) via
$\text{out} = \text{gate} \cdot W_{down}^{T}$ using the same CPU-pre-decoded weight
format as Stages~2a/2b. A down-projection row has 32 blocks rather than 8, so each
iteration processes $K=2$ rows in 4 sequential groups of 8 blocks: 16 MAC lanes
(8 blocks $\times$ 2 rows), 256 cycles per group at $II=1$. The producer fetches the two
rows one after the other, 320 words each (64 header words, then 256 nibble words), and splits each nibble word into four 32-bit
BRAM tiles, one per group (8 tiles in total); the 128-bit wide-tile layout used in
Stages~2a/2b was tried here and could not be routed (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

As in Stages~2a/2b, the loop body is a dataflow region. The csynth report gives 960
cycles for the two-row load and 1,248 for the MAC, so an iteration costs 1,249 cycles
and the stage is MAC-bound. The total is 1,282,062 cycles (5.13\,ms), which is
$1024\times1249$ plus the 2,051-cycle output write and the pipeline fill. This sets the
dataflow interval of the whole IP.

The choice of $K=2$ follows from the overlap. The previous version processed four rows
per iteration: its four-row load took 1,908 cycles and its MAC 1,243, run back to back
(3,165 cycles per iteration, 1,622,602 in total). With overlap, $K=4$ would still be
load-bound, because the four-row load is longer than the MAC and the MAC array would
idle for part of every iteration. Halving $K$ makes the load (960 cycles) shorter than
the MAC (1,248), and the DSP and logic it frees pay for the double buffers (DSP 286 to
238 in csynth). At the csynth latency the stage streams its matrix at about 2.04\,GB/s
(51\% of the port peak) and performs about $3.3\times10^{9}$ MAC/s, 82\% of its 16-lane
peak (derived).
