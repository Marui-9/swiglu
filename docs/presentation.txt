Speaker Notes — Tiny Acceleration of Liquid Foundation Models

Slide 1 — Title
Good morning / afternoon, everyone. This presentation is about a hardware-software co-design project whose goal is to make a modern large-language-model run at conversational speed — meaning fast enough to hold a real-time spoken dialogue — on a small, embedded device that draws only a few watts of power. The work is a collaboration between the University of Trento and STMicroelectronics, and it sits at the intersection of three fields: edge computing, AI inference, and FPGA digital design. By the end I hope to convince you that, with the right choice of model and the right choice of what to accelerate, you can get surprisingly close to real-time performance on hardware that most people would not consider capable of running a billion-parameter language model.


Slide 2 — Agenda
The talk follows a natural engineering progression. We start with the motivation: why would you want to run a language model on an edge device at all, and how hard is that in practice? We then look at the state of the art to understand what others have done before. From there, we explain how we chose the specific model we worked with, and how we profiled it to understand where the computation actually goes. The second half of the talk covers the proposed system — how the FPGA accelerator is designed, how it interfaces with the CPU, and what performance it achieves. We close with a comparison against related work and a roadmap for future improvements.


Slide 3 — Background & Motivation (section divider)
This first section sets the stage. Before talking about any hardware, it is worth explaining why edge deployment of conversational AI is an interesting and non-trivial problem.


Slide 4 — Why Edge AI for Conversational Audio?
When you interact with a voice assistant today, your speech is almost certainly sent to a cloud server, processed there, and the response is streamed back. This works, but it creates three classes of problems. First, latency: even a fast network adds tens to hundreds of milliseconds of round-trip delay on top of the model's own compute time, and human perception of conversation is sensitive to delays beyond about 500 ms. Second, privacy: speech carries biometric information — your voice, your accent, your health state, the content of what you say — and sending it to an external server is a non-trivial data exposure. Third, reliability: a device that depends on a cloud connection loses its conversational capability in low-connectivity environments. Running the entire pipeline on-device eliminates all three problems. The challenge is that the models required for high-quality conversational audio — an automatic speech recognition front-end, a language model, and a text-to-speech back-end — are large and compute-hungry by embedded-hardware standards. The core question of this work is therefore: how close to real-time can we get, on modest hardware, with a state-of-the-art model?


Slide 5 — State of the Art (section divider)
To position the contribution, let us briefly survey what the community has done in related areas.


Slide 6 — From TinyML to Generative Edge AI
Edge AI has evolved in two distinct phases. The first phase, roughly 2020 to 2023, is what is usually called TinyML: deploying convolutional and recurrent neural networks on microcontrollers for tasks like keyword spotting, gesture recognition, or anomaly detection. These models are small — typically under a million parameters — and they fit in hundreds of kilobytes of memory. Since 2024, the edge has moved toward what we call Generative Edge AI: deploying quantized transformer-based models, also known as small language models or SLMs, that retain the reasoning and generation capabilities of large models while fitting within tighter resource envelopes. Techniques such as quantization (representing weights in 4 or 8 bits instead of 32), pruning, knowledge distillation, and hardware-aware architecture search have all contributed to making this possible. For the specific case of speech, the dominant pipeline remains modular: an ASR model converts audio to text, a language model generates a text response, and a TTS model synthesises the audio output. Each stage can be optimised independently. More recently, end-to-end models that fuse all three stages into a single network have appeared, reducing inter-stage latency at the cost of a larger single-model footprint. Platforms like the Raspberry Pi 4B have become the common evaluation platform for on-device speech, hosting ASR engines such as Faster-Whisper. But while these boards handle the ASR front-end, a capable conversational pipeline aggregates three or four models totalling several billion parameters, and the language-model stage is bound by memory bandwidth — so a commodity CPU struggles to meet the 200-to-500-millisecond per-stage latency budget. The binding constraint at the edge is latency and bandwidth, not raw power.


Slide 7 — Model Selection & Profiling (section divider)
Given this landscape, the first concrete decision is which model to target. This section describes how that choice was made systematically.


Slide 8 — Speech Model Profiling (GGUF)
Rather than picking a model by intuition, we profiled a representative set of speech and multimodal models to understand their deployment requirements quantitatively. The tool used — gguf-parser-go — extracts, from standardised model checkpoint files, the parameter count, RAM footprint, and estimated memory bandwidth demand. Figure 1 shows the result as a scatter plot: each bubble is a model, its position encodes parameter count and RAM usage, its size encodes estimated throughput in tera-operations per second. The key insight from this chart is that parameter count alone is a poor predictor of edge feasibility. Some models with 7 to 9 billion parameters exceed both the memory and bandwidth limits of embedded platforms despite offering strong conversational quality. To be deployable on a device drawing 3 to 10 watts with 4 GB of shared memory, a model needs to land in the lower-left region of this chart — small parameter count, low RAM, moderate bandwidth demand.


Slide 9 — The Choice of LFM2.5-Audio-1.5B-Q4
The profiling points clearly to one candidate: LFM2.5-Audio-1.5B-Q4, a model developed by Liquid AI. In its 4-bit quantized form it occupies only 0.29 GB of RAM — the smallest footprint among all models profiled — while requiring roughly 3.9 GB/s of memory bandwidth and offering a competitive 1.18 billion parameters. It is also an end-to-end architecture: a single model handles audio encoding, language modelling, and speech synthesis, eliminating the inter-stage handoff latency of a modular pipeline. Crucially, its 1.2-billion-parameter language backbone — the component that does the heavy reasoning — was explicitly co-designed for efficient CPU inference under 4-bit quantization. This makes it a natural, well-grounded starting point for hardware acceleration.


Slide 10 — CPU Execution Profile of the Language Backbone
Once the model is chosen, the next question is: where does the time go? To answer this, we instrumented the llama.cpp inference engine — the standard open-source CPU inference framework for these models — to measure how long each architectural block takes per token, on a quad-core ARM Cortex-A53 at 1.3 GHz. The results in Table 1 are stark. The MatMul + SwiGLU feed-forward block alone accounts for 68.1% of total inference time. The next largest contributors are the final linear projection (11%) and a gated short convolution specific to the LFM architecture (10.4%). Attention — which in larger models is often the dominant cost — contributes only 8.9% here, because for a 1.2B-parameter model the attention matrices are small relative to the feed-forward layers. This profiling result directly motivates the design strategy: rather than trying to accelerate every operation in the model, we focus exclusively on the feed-forward block. Amdahl's Law gives us an upper bound on the benefit: even if the feed-forward block ran instantaneously, the non-accelerated 32% of the workload caps the theoretical speedup at about 3.1×. In practice, the non-FFN operations take around 126 ms per token on this CPU, setting a hard throughput ceiling of roughly 8 tokens per second regardless of how fast we make the feed-forward path.


Slide 11 — Proposed System: FPGA Offload (section divider)
With the bottleneck identified, we can now describe the system we built to address it.


Slide 12 — Workload Partitioning
The system is heterogeneous: the FPGA handles the feed-forward MatMul + SwiGLU block for each of the 16 transformer layers in the model, while the CPU handles everything else — attention, normalisation, residual additions, the gated short convolution, and the final linear projection. This partitioning is deliberate. Offloading only the dominant kernel means the FPGA design is compact enough to fit on a small device, the integration with the existing inference framework is surgical rather than a full rewrite, and the fallback to CPU-only execution is trivially available by flipping an environment variable. The trade-off is that the non-accelerated operations become the new ceiling, which Amdahl's Law already told us is around 8 tokens per second.


Slide 13 — Orchestration of the Computational Offload
The handoff between CPU and FPGA is managed inside a modified version of llama.cpp. When the inference engine builds the computation graph for a token, it recognises the feed-forward block and replaces it with a fused hardware operator. At execution time, the following sequence happens: first, the three weight matrices for the feed-forward block — gate projection, up projection, and down projection — are loaded into a DMA-coherent shared memory buffer the first time they are needed, then cached there permanently. This is important: with 16 layers each holding about 30 MB of weights, re-transferring weights every token would dominate the runtime. Second, the current activation vector (the 2048-dimensional float representing the current token state) is quantized from 32-bit floating point to 8-bit integers on the CPU. Third, the CPU writes the addresses and scaling factors to the FPGA's control registers over an AXI-Lite interface, asserts a start signal, and then suspends itself waiting for an interrupt. The FPGA executes the full feed-forward computation, writes the 32-bit floating point result back to the shared buffer, and raises an interrupt. The CPU then resumes and passes the output to the next node in the graph. If the environment variable LLAMA_SWIHW is set to 0, or if the tensor shapes do not match, execution falls back to the standard CPU path with no changes required.


Slide 14 — Accelerator Design (section divider)
Let us now go inside the FPGA and look at how the feed-forward computation is implemented.


Slide 15 — MatMul + SwiGLU Workload
The feed-forward block in a transformer — specifically the SwiGLU variant used here — involves three matrix-vector multiplications and a non-linear gating operation. Concretely: the input activation vector (2048 elements) is projected up to 8192 dimensions by two separate weight matrices (the gate and up projections), a SiLU non-linearity is applied element-wise to the gate output, the two 8192-dimensional vectors are multiplied element-wise, and the result is projected back down to 2048 dimensions by the down projection matrix. The FPGA implements this as a five-stage HLS dataflow pipeline — meaning each stage runs concurrently, passing data to the next as soon as it is ready, targeting an initiation interval of 1 (i.e. the pipeline can accept a new input every clock cycle in principle). The weights are stored in 4-bit quantized format (Q4_K), and activations are in 8-bit integer. The multiplications therefore produce 12-bit products, which are accumulated in 32-bit integer chains. There are 32 parallel multiply-accumulate chains running simultaneously. The achievable peak arithmetic throughput is 8 GOps/s, but — and this is the central finding of the accelerator analysis — the design is not limited by arithmetic capacity. It is limited by how fast data can be loaded from off-chip DDR memory. The projection weight matrices are approximately 10 MB each per layer, far too large to fit in the FPGA's on-chip memory (about 3.4 MB total on this device — 27 Mb of combined block, ultra, and distributed RAM). Every token therefore requires streaming the weights from DDR, and memory bandwidth, not compute, is the binding constraint. Stages 2a and 2b (the gate and up projections) achieve 41% of the FPGA's AXI access bandwidth to DDR. Stage 5 (the down projection) achieves 38% and is the slowest stage at 6.49 ms — it therefore sets the pipeline interval, meaning the entire dataflow pipeline can only produce one result every 6.49 ms, regardless of how fast the other stages are. In FPGA design terminology, when a dataflow pipeline has one stage that is slower than all others, that stage is called the DATAFLOW bottleneck: it determines the throughput of the whole pipeline the same way the slowest link determines the speed of a chain. Stage 5 is slower than 2a/2b not because of different arithmetic, but because its weight matrix has 32 blocks per row instead of 8. The reason is dimensional. The gate and up projections contract over the 2048-element embedding dimension, so each of their weight rows holds 2048 values — and since the Q4_K format packs 256 weights per block, that is 8 blocks per row. The down projection does the opposite: it contracts over the 8192-element feed-forward dimension to map the gated activation back to 2048 outputs, so each of its rows holds 8192 values, which is 32 Q4_K blocks. This four-times-longer row forces a different, less efficient memory access pattern that cannot be merged into a single burst transfer.


Slide 16 — Bottlenecks
To make the bandwidth limitation concrete: even though the FPGA fabric has 32 parallel MAC chains capable of 8 GOps/s of arithmetic, the sustained effective throughput is only 2.58 GOps/s — about 32% of peak — because the arithmetic units spend more time waiting for data to arrive from DDR than actually computing. This is a regime known as memory-bound execution, and it is common in autoregressive language model decoding, where the model processes one token at a time and the weight matrices must therefore be streamed fresh for each token rather than reused across a large batch. The bottleneck is therefore not a failure of the hardware design per se; it is a structural consequence of the workload and the memory capacity of the device.


Slide 17 — FPGA Resource Utilization on Kria KV260 (ZU5EV)
The target platform is the AMD-Xilinx Kria KV260, a development kit built around the Zynq UltraScale+ ZU5EV system-on-chip. This chip integrates a quad-core ARM Cortex-A53 processor and a programmable FPGA fabric on the same die, sharing a unified 4 GB DDR4 memory pool. The bar chart shows how much of the FPGA's on-chip resources are consumed by the feed-forward accelerator. LUT (Look-Up Table) usage is 87% — nearly at the device's limit. Flip-flop usage is 50%, BRAM (block RAM) is 32%, URAM (ultra RAM) is 13%, and DSP slice usage is only 20%. The high LUT occupancy — and notably the low DSP usage — reflects a key architectural detail: the core multiplication in this design is a 4-bit weight times an 8-bit activation, yielding a 12-bit product. The DSP48E2 slices on this FPGA are optimised for 18×27-bit multiplications; using them for 4×8-bit products is inefficient, so the HLS compiler maps the inner-product logic to LUTs instead. The 20% DSP usage comes entirely from the floating-point accumulation of scale factors. The 87% LUT occupancy means there is essentially no room for additional hardware blocks on this device — any further acceleration would require a larger FPGA.


Slide 18 — Experimental Results (section divider)
With the system fully described, we can now look at what it actually achieves.


Slide 19 — Throughput & Efficiency: FPGA+CPU vs CPU Only
Table 2 reports the measured throughput in tokens per second and performance per watt across four thread counts (T1 through T4, meaning 1 to 4 CPU threads active) and two configurations (CPU Only and FPGA+CPU). A few things to note about the setup: the CPU-Only baseline uses the Q4_K_M quantization of the model (a mixed-precision variant where 8 of 16 down-projection layers are in Q6_K and the rest in Q4_K), while the FPGA+CPU path uses a uniformly re-quantized Q4_K model across all layers. This is necessary because the Q6_K format — which packs weights in 6-bit groups with a more complex header structure — cannot be efficiently decoded in the FPGA's LUT-based datapath at the current device's routing capacity. The prefill phase refers to processing the prompt (the input tokens), and the decode phase refers to generating the response tokens one by one. The two highlighted results in blue are the thread counts where FPGA+CPU achieves better performance per watt than CPU Only: T1 and T2. At T1, FPGA+CPU prefill is 0.431 t/s/W versus 0.275 for CPU Only — a 57% gain, with decode showing a comparable +44%. At T2, the gain narrows to about 10% for both prefill and decode. At T3 and T4, the CPU-Only configuration is superior.


Slide 20 — Results Analysis
The pattern in the data tells a clear story about when FPGA offload helps and when it does not. At low thread counts (T1, T2), the CPU is the bottleneck: it cannot feed the attention and normalisation operations fast enough to keep the pipeline busy, and the FPGA offload of the feed-forward block reduces the total time the CPU spends on the dominant kernel. The accelerator is effective because host-side contention is low and the fixed overhead of each CPU-FPGA handoff can be amortised. At high thread counts (T3, T4), the CPU is fast enough to process all operations — including the feed-forward block — at a rate that rivals or exceeds the FPGA's contribution. Meanwhile, the overhead of the handoff (quantizing the activation, programming the control registers, waiting for the interrupt, copying the result) becomes a significant fraction of the per-token budget. At T4, all four A53 cores are competing for scheduler time with the interrupt handler, which actively degrades decode throughput to below the T3 value. The practical implication is that the FPGA offload is most valuable when the surrounding pipeline — ASR and TTS stages — also needs CPU resources. In that scenario, reducing the language model's thread demand from three or four to two frees cores for the other stages, which directly helps the end-to-end latency.


Slide 21 — Obtained Throughput
The design achieves 61% of its own theoretical maximum. That maximum — 3.6 tokens per second — is itself bounded by three hardware-level constraints identified during development. First, the AXI burst serialisation overhead: each DDR burst incurs approximately 45 cycles of setup time before data starts flowing. Merging multiple weight rows into a single large burst (as done in stages 2a/2b) reduces this overhead dramatically; stage 5 cannot apply the same strategy because its weight rows are four times longer, forcing sequential per-row bursts. Second, the HLS memory scheduler cannot issue four simultaneous writes to the nibble-storage banks within the same pipeline clock cycle. The reason is that stage 5 processes four weight rows in parallel, and because the nibbles are held in narrow single-port buffers, each 128-bit word fetched from DDR must be split and written into four separate buffers in the same cycle — four memory writes per pipeline stage. Vitis HLS 2025.1 will not pack four writes into one cycle, so the load loop runs at an initiation interval of four instead of one, quadrupling the time stage 5 spends loading weights. Crucially this is an operation-count limit, not a physical one: cyclic partitioning that proves the four banks are independent does not help, and swapping the storage between BRAM and URAM changes nothing — roughly ten different architectural formulations all converged on the same wall. The gate and up projections escape this by storing each 128-bit word verbatim in a single wide buffer, which is one write per cycle with no fanout; stage 5 cannot, because its 32-blocks-per-row access pattern would require 32 simultaneous block, header, and gate reads in the multiply-accumulate, whose interconnect density the ZU5EV cannot route in that wide form — so the down path must fall back to narrow tiles and the four-way write fanout. A hand-written RTL datapath could fan out to four banks at once, so the ceiling here is the high-level synthesis tool, not the silicon. Third, the ZU5EV's routing fabric cannot simultaneously carry all the signals required for a fully-unrolled output MAC array at 250 MHz — the interconnect density exceeds the device's physical routing capacity. These three constraints together cap the design at 3.6 t/s, and 61% of that ceiling is what the deployed implementation reaches. These ceilings are also the roadmap for future work.


Slide 22 — Discussion (section divider)
With the results in hand, let us now step back and interpret them in the context of the original goals.


Slide 23 — Real-Time Latency Compliance
Recall the latency target defined at the start: per-token generation time should fall between 200 and 500 ms for the language model stage alone, so that the full pipeline (ASR + LM + TTS) has a chance of remaining within the sub-second budget expected for responsive spoken dialogue. The table on this slide maps each operating point to its decode latency. Of the KV260 configurations, four enter the sub-500 ms window: T3 and T4 CPU-Only (424 and 433 ms), and T2 and T3 FPGA+CPU (452 and 488 ms). No configuration reaches the 200 ms "instantaneous" threshold. The critical advantage of the FPGA+CPU path is that it achieves sub-500 ms decode with only two active CPU threads, whereas CPU-Only needs three or four. This thread savings matters: a complete conversational pipeline must simultaneously run ASR and TTS on the same four cores. If the language model monopolises three or four of them, the other stages are starved. The FPGA offload effectively buys back two cores for the surrounding pipeline.


Slide 24 — Why Are the Gains Limited?
The gains are bounded by a combination of factors at different levels. At the hardware level: the FPGA on the ZU5EV is nearly full (87% LUTs), the FPGA's AXI access bandwidth to DDR is capped at 4 GB/s (a single 128-bit port at 250 MHz) while DDR is shared between the CPU and FPGA, and the weight matrices are too large for on-chip storage, making DDR streaming unavoidable. At the micro-architecture level: AXI burst setup overhead, the HLS scheduler's write-fanout limit, and routing congestion all contribute to the gap between peak and achieved throughput. At the system level: the per-token synchronous handoff between CPU and FPGA — program registers, start, wait for interrupt, copy result — adds fixed latency that does not amortise with sequence length and grows worse under multi-threaded CPU load. None of these is a consequence of poor design choices; they are the structural limits of selective acceleration on a resource-constrained device. The results therefore characterise the ceiling of what this approach can achieve on ZU5EV-class hardware, and establish the baseline from which future work can measure progress.


Slide 25 — Comparison with Other Work on the Same Platform
Three prior designs on the Kria KV260 are compared in Table 3. A Tiny Transformer for ECG classification (6,600 parameters) fits its entire pipeline on-chip, eliminating DDR weight fetching entirely and running in a compute-bound regime where the arithmetic units are the bottleneck — a fundamentally different situation from ours. A DistilBERT self-attention accelerator uses a two-level tiling strategy to keep the Q, K, and V projection matrices within on-chip BRAM, sustaining 91% of peak arithmetic throughput with 83% DSP utilisation. This is only possible because DistilBERT's attention matrices are small enough to tile into 7 MB of on-chip memory. The LFM2.5 feed-forward weights total about 30 MB per layer across the three projections — nearly nine times the entire on-chip memory — so tiling the weights is not an option; DDR streaming is unavoidable. A BRAM-banked matrix multiplication accelerator achieves 59% of its 22.83 GOps/s peak, identifying PS-side (processor system side) data handling as its dominant bottleneck — the same bottleneck we encounter, at a larger scale. Our design reaches 32–34% of arithmetic peak, consistent with being in a more severely bandwidth-limited regime.


Slide 26 — Comparison: KV260 vs STM32MP257F-DK
To understand the value of the FPGA fabric specifically, we also benchmarked LFM2.5-1.2B on the STM32MP257F-DK, an ST development board built around a dual-core ARM Cortex-A35 running at up to 1.5 GHz. This is a device with no programmable accelerator — purely a CPU. At T2 (both cores active), the STM32 achieves 1.28 tokens per second decode throughput, with a total board power of approximately 0.80 W. The KV260 FPGA+CPU at T2 achieves 2.21 tokens per second — a 1.7× speedup — but draws 5.15 W. In terms of performance per watt, the STM32 is 3.7× more efficient (1.60 t/s/W versus 0.43 t/s/W). So why is the FPGA offload justified? Latency. The STM32 at T2 has a per-token decode latency of 781 ms — outside the sub-500 ms real-time window. The KV260 FPGA+CPU at T2 delivers 452 ms — just inside it. No amount of efficiency advantage matters if the device cannot meet the real-time deadline at all. The FPGA offload is not justified on power grounds; it is justified because it enables a latency target that the CPU alone cannot reach on this device class.


Slide 27 — Conclusions & Future Work (section divider)
Let us summarise what was achieved and where the work goes next.


Slide 28 — Conclusions
This work demonstrated that selectively offloading the feed-forward MatMul + SwiGLU block of a 1.2-billion-parameter language model to an FPGA accelerator yields meaningful efficiency gains at low CPU thread counts, and — more importantly — enables sub-500 ms per-token decode latency with only two active CPU threads on a 5-watt embedded platform. The integration is clean: it hooks into the existing llama.cpp inference stack with minimal changes and falls back to CPU execution transparently. The cases where FPGA+CPU does not outperform CPU-Only are not failures of the design but reflections of structural constraints — memory bandwidth, orchestration overhead, and FPGA routing limits — that are now quantified and understood. The design already operates at 87% LUT occupancy on the target device, so further gains on the same hardware require memory-path improvements rather than more parallelism.


Slide 29 — Future Work & Roadmap
Five directions are identified. Memory-centric optimisation: the current design achieves 38–41% of DDR peak bandwidth; tiling, double-buffering, and layout restructuring could push this higher. Asynchronous scheduling: replacing the current synchronous interrupt-driven handoff with a pipelined CPU-FPGA interaction would reduce the fixed per-token overhead and improve scalability at higher thread counts. This is the one direction that trades away the design's drop-in compatibility, however: ggml's graph executor runs strictly node-by-node with a barrier between operations, so overlapping FPGA execution with CPU work would require either modifying llama.cpp's scheduler or moving the synchronization into the kernel/driver interrupt path — both of which break the transparent, environment-variable-gated fallback that the rest of the integration preserves. Lighter-weight alternatives such as register spin-polling were tested but yielded only marginal latency improvement while raising power enough to reduce tokens-per-watt below the interrupt-driven baseline. Extending hardware coverage: the final linear projection (11%) and gated short convolution (10%) together account for about 21% of remaining runtime; accelerating them would require additional FPGA fabric, which is not available on the KV260 but would be on a larger device. Device migration: the ZU7EV, the next member of the Zynq UltraScale+ family, offers approximately 230,000 LUTs versus the ZU5EV's 117,000, which would provide enough routing headroom to fully unroll the output MAC array and merge the AXI burst calls that currently serialise stage 5. Full pipeline integration: the ultimate goal is co-deploying ASR, language model, and TTS within the same power envelope on a single SoC, which requires all three stages to share the same four CPU cores and the FPGA fabric without starving each other.
